Inspirations

For players who know the hobby's history, FRH comes out of several traditions at once: old campaign-world depth, free-form role assumption, information asymmetry, sharp scene pacing, occult investigation, and a conviction that people matter more than plot outline.

The Short Version

The GM's own compressed comparison is that Followers of the Right Hand is something like Call of Cthulhu1 with Wild Cards2-style Aces and Joker-Aces in an X-Files, or B.P.R.D., investigation3, run through a Charlie's Angels4-like organizational structure with Edgar in the Charlie role, all while the party manages some of the volatile interpersonal chemistry you might expect from The League of Extraordinary Gentlemen5, only inside a hidden supernatural universe. In a way, it is also a bit like the original Ghostbusters movie or The Real Ghostbusters animated series.

That is not a mechanical formula so much as a tonal map. FRH has some Call of Cthulhu dread in its bones, but the investigators are more capable than classic near-helpless investigators. It has pulp and ensemble energy, but it is less interested in invulnerability than in meaningful action under pressure.

If you want the longer hobby lineage behind that blend, Meet the GM expands the list of games, campaign traditions, and table instincts that helped shape FRH.

Tekumel and Braunstein / Blackmoor

One major root is the old campaign-world tradition: the sense that the setting exists in depth, has its own pressures, and is not merely a stage built for one plot. Tekumel6 contributes that feeling of dense inherited reality, deep history, hidden institutions, and the conviction that social and cosmological structures are older than the players' immediate needs. Braunstein7 and Blackmoor8 contribute something equally important: the idea that roleplaying becomes vivid when players are given charged situations, distinct roles, incomplete information, and the freedom to act rather than being marched through predetermined outcomes.

That matters because FRH is not trying to tell one fixed story at the players. It is trying to place strange, capable people inside a world with real depth and let the meaning emerge through their choices, misreadings, recoveries, and commitments.

Amber Diceless, Paranoia, and Feng Shui

From Amber Diceless9, FRH inherits a narrative-first instinct: people, motives, and interpretive pressure matter more than procedural grind for its own sake. From Paranoia10, it borrows the usefulness of information asymmetry, unequal knowledge, and the way secrecy can shape tone without automatically turning the table into a betrayal machine. From Feng Shui11, it draws some of its plotting and pacing sense: how to cut between pressure modes, when to let a scene breathe, when to turn sharply, and how to give action or revelation enough shape to feel memorable instead of merely efficient.

Taken together, those influences help explain why FRH can be talk-heavy without feeling static and dangerous without feeling joyless. The game values scene rhythm, withheld knowledge, and the dramatic charge of people acting before the whole map is visible.

The Larger Blend

FRH also lives near several familiar reference points without collapsing into any one of them. It has occult-investigation energy that will feel adjacent to Call of Cthulhu, but the investigators are often closer to unusually burdened Aces or Joker-Aces in the Wild Cards sense than to ordinary victims. The term comes from the original Bantam Books run of the shared-world series from 1987 to 1993, where superhuman transformation does not just grant powers; it also changes status, body, social legibility, and the shape of a life.12

For readers who do not know the terms: in Wild Cards, an Ace is someone whose transformation expresses as a powerful or unusual ability, while a Joker is someone whose transformation is more visibly extreme, bodily strange, or socially difficult. A Joker-Ace sits in between or across those lines. FRH uses those terms loosely as a tonal comparison, not as literal setting canon.

If you want a concrete example pass instead of only the abstract comparison, the packet now has a short companion page using FRH sample characters to illustrate those expressions more directly: Ace and Joker Expressions in FRH.

It also has an episodic mission structure that sometimes resembles a secret-organization investigation game, while the group dynamics can feel more like a volatile, gifted ensemble whose members do not always agree on what the right thing to do is. Around and behind all that sits a hidden cosmology, a long damaged history, and the possibility that the universe is not only tragic or hierarchical but faintly absurd in ways no one inside it can fully master.

That is a long way of saying: FRH is a synthesis game. It is interested in mystery, power, institutions, danger, character, moral choice, and the possibility that reality is layered enough that even good explanations remain partial. The game takes all of that seriously without forgetting that people still joke, hesitate, bluff, misread, and surprise one another while they live through it.

Notes

  1. Call of Cthulhu is Chaosium's landmark horror-investigation RPG.
  2. Wild Cards is the long-running shared-world series edited by George R. R. Martin and Melinda M. Snodgrass.
  3. The X-Files, or B.P.R.D., are the obvious touchstones for casework, hidden structures, and investigative escalation.
  4. Charlie's Angels matters here mainly as an organizational metaphor: a distant handler, dispatched agents, and mission framing.
  5. The League of Extraordinary Gentlemen is useful as a shorthand for volatile ensemble chemistry among unusually gifted people.
  6. Tekumel points toward deep setting history and a world that feels older than the current adventure.
  7. Braunstein is one of the hobby's earliest role-assumption roots.
  8. Blackmoor points toward persistent campaign-world play rather than one-shot scenario structure.
  9. Amber Diceless is a strong reference point for narrative-first pressure and character-centered confrontation.
  10. Paranoia is referenced here for information asymmetry, not for comedy tone or player elimination.
  11. Feng Shui is referenced here for pacing and scene momentum rather than genre similarity.
  12. The first Bantam Books phase of Wild Cards ran from 1987 to 1993 and established the Ace / Joker terminology that later readers still inherit.
