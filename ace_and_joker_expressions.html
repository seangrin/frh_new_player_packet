Ace and Joker Expressions in FRH

FRH uses Ace, Joker, and Joker-Ace as loose tonal comparisons, not as literal setting law. This page exists to show what those expressions can look like in actual FRH character design.

What These Terms Mean Here

In stricter Wild Cards parlance, an Ace is still recognizably human in presentation. The powers may be dramatic, but they do not compromise the person's ordinary human appearance. An Joker-Ace has real power, but also has a physical presentation, bodily strangeness, or visible instability that makes going unnoticed in ordinary society nearly impossible. A Joker has powers that are useless, self-harming, or socially catastrophic enough that the person is rendered less able to live effectively rather than more able. Classic Jokers are often deformed, rejected, or made unlivable by what happened to them.

FRH does not use those terms as literal setting canon. It uses them as a way to think about what a gift costs in social legibility, ordinary life, and bodily presentation. The same person may look manageable on paper and still produce fear, panic, or exclusion the moment the full truth becomes visible.

Ace Expressions

  • Lucien Wei Mercier: a polished example of unusual capability that still presents as workable, legible, and socially functional. The power expression reads as gifted rather than destabilizing.
  • Matthew Bridges: a strong example of gifted range that still reads cleanly to the outside world. Wealth, training, and unusual leverage make him powerful without making him socially illegible.

Joker-Ace Expressions

  • Gabriel Voss: powerful, haunted, and visibly wrong enough that he does not pass as simply gifted. The physical and spiritual pressure are part of the presentation, not merely a hidden note in the build.
  • Adrian Draik: capable, but bodily and temperamentally strange in a way that keeps ordinary society from ever receiving him as cleanly human and ordinary. His flaws are part of how the strangeness lands.
  • Siegfried Vane: competence does not save him from reading as alarming. His force of presentation, burden, and intensity make him harder to place inside normal life than a conventional Ace.
  • Mila Johannson: age, damage, and old power all register on the body and the personality. She is effective, but she does not move through the world as a cleanly integrated, applause-ready superhuman.
  • Joe Bob Gray and Joe Bob Gray (Werewolf Form): Joe Bob can seem like an Ace until the werewolf reality arrives. At that point the transformation does not produce cheers. It produces terror, loss of ordinary legibility, and a form the public cannot absorb without panic.
  • Edwin March and Edwin March (Monster Form): Edwin's alternate state is exactly the kind of thing that prevents a character from remaining a socially smooth Ace. Whatever his usefulness, the transformation crosses into visible horror, and ordinary society would answer with fear rather than admiration.

Joker Expression

Tiberius Sinclair is the clearest Joker example once he has gone insane. He still has power. He still has remarkable occult value. But he is no longer able to live effectively, integrate reliably, or inhabit the world in a stable and socially workable way. That is the important distinction here. The problem is not that he is weak. The problem is that he has been rendered unable to carry his gifts as a functioning life.

In classic Wild Cards terms, this is the lane where a person resembles a Joker more than a triumphant superhuman. Think of someone like Snot Man: a body made horrific and socially unlivable, powers that do not translate into an ordinary functioning existence, and a life made harder rather than freer. Tiberius is not the same case physically, but after his collapse he belongs closer to that tragic logic than to the Ace fantasy.

Why This Matters for Character Creation

The point is not to force your investigator into a label. The point is to help you notice what kind of social and supernatural pressure your design creates. If your character is more Ace-like, the interesting question is often what hidden cost, secrecy, or burden complicates that clean surface. If your character is more Joker-like, the interesting question is how they remain playable, relational, and useful inside a world that does not make easy room for them.

FRH gets stronger when a character's gifts, body, social readability, and risks all seem to belong to the same person rather than being optimized in isolation. These examples are here to show range, not to close doors.
