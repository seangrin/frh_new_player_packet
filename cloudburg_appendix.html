Why This Appendix Exists

The main Cloudburg chapter is there to give a new player the feel of the setting quickly. This appendix is for when you want the place to become sharper. It is still player-facing, but it is willing to be more specific about history, geography, class, and local habits. If the main chapter tells you why Cloudburg works, this appendix helps explain why it feels the way it does.

Cloudburg is not merely eerie. It is organized. It has routes in and out, institutions that preserve respectability, labor that keeps the town alive, and older layers of meaning that no modern town government truly controls. That combination is why cases can begin there so naturally. Pressure already exists in the place before any haunting, relic, or secret society enters the scene.

Location, Access, and Scale

Cloudburg sits in southern Colorado along the Piedra River, roughly eight miles southeast of Chimney Rock. In practical terms, it is remote without being unreachable. The river, the rail line, and the passable routes through the southern Rockies make the town viable. They do not make it easy. That difference matters. People can arrive, goods can move, students and professors can drift in from elsewhere, but the town still keeps the feeling of a place held together by terrain rather than by convenience.

The population in 1923 is roughly 1,700. That is large enough for institutions to matter and small enough that reputation travels fast. A banker, a mine foreman, a professor, a hotel keeper, and a psychic medium can all belong to different social worlds while still living inside the same rumor system.

Connor MacLeod and the Shape of the Town

Cloudburg did not arise by accident. The town's founding story centers on Connor MacLeod, a Scotsman from Lochaber who came west after studying geology and metallurgy. He was not simply lucky enough to find gold. He was educated enough to know what he was looking at, ambitious enough to take the risk, and forceful enough to turn a mining settlement founded in 1861 into a county seat.

That matters because it means Cloudburg carries intention in its bones. The town grew around extraction, trust, and control. MacLeod's mine created wealth. The bank stabilized it. The county structure legitimized it. The university refined it. Even after MacLeod's disappearance and the death of his wife Angela, the trust remained. The town still lives inside the structure he built.

Public History and Local Memory

The region bears Spanish names and much older indigenous memory. The Ute presence predates both American statehood and formal settlement. That older layer is not decorative. It is one reason the land never feels fully explained by the mining narrative alone.

Local public history presents Cloudburg as a triumph of frontier ambition, mining wealth, and civic improvement. Local memory is less neat. It remembers executions along Fila de Horca. It remembers that some families do not go near the lake after dark. It remembers that certain things are easier to leave unnamed than to disprove. FRH works well in Cloudburg because both kinds of history are true at once.

Class, Elevation, and Daily Life

The main chapter says that status follows elevation. The appendix version is blunter: Cloudburg's social order is legible in the walk uphill. The rail line, freight, ore, stock, boarding houses, and rough practical life belong to the lower district. The middle tier contains the banking and managerial world. Higher still sits Isla Oro University, where ideas, imported luxuries, and academic habits make the town feel larger than it is.

This produces a setting where investigators can move through visibly different kinds of pressure in the same afternoon. A case can begin with a worker's testimony, shift into a bank office or trustee conversation, and end in a university archive or lake expedition without the social fabric ever feeling arbitrary. Cloudburg has enough stratification to make every witness, favor, and trespass feel situated.

The elite remain mostly white families with roots in New England, the British Isles, Germany, and France. The working population is split between white and Hispanic labor, with a small Black population and some Ute families. These are not trivia points. They affect who is heard, who is dismissed, who is watched, and who already knows that the respectable story is incomplete.

Useful Hard Facts

  • Settlement founded by Connor MacLeod in 1861.
  • Cloudburg becomes county seat when Colorado reaches statehood in 1876.
  • Isla Oro University opens on August 26, 1881 with 13 students.
  • By 1923 the university supports roughly 17 full-time professors and about 200 students.
  • Main local pillars are mining, banking, transportation, and the university.
  • MacLeod Bank & Trust helps anchor the town's respectable and financial center.
  • Lago Brillante and Isla Oro matter both geographically and symbolically.

Lago Brillante, Isla Oro, and the Golden Fruit Tree

Southwest of town lies Lago Brillante. At its center is Isla Oro, named by Spanish explorers after the island's apple tree. The Ute name for that tree is older. The public version of the story treats the tree as an anomaly. The local version treats it as a warning.

The upper town can afford to find the island picturesque. It appears in sketches and photographs. The university can afford to call it a curiosity. Workers, older Hispanic households, Ute families, and others whose traditions are less eager to mock inherited caution are more likely to treat the lake as a place best respected from a distance, especially after dark.

This split is useful for FRH because it tells you something important about the setting. Cloudburg is not full of fools. It is full of people who interpret the same place through different obligations, traditions, and risks. That is often more interesting than simple belief versus disbelief.

Edgar, Fila de Horca, and the Edge of Town

Fila de Horca Boulevard keeps one foot in civic life and one in remembered violence. The gallows are gone, but the name survives, and so does the social imagination attached to it. Edgar Von Haupt's house sits at the end of that line for good reason. It is near enough to town to matter and separate enough to preserve privacy, ritual, and security.

For new players, Edgar's position is useful to understand in spatial terms. He is not at the center of respectable town life, and he is not out in the wilderness either. He occupies a deliberate threshold. That is one reason he works so well as a packet guide into the setting.

What a Player Should Carry Away

Cloudburg is a place where the supernatural lands in a world that already has hierarchy, labor, memory, and contested explanations. It is remote, but not blank. It is civilized, but not settled. It is beautiful, but not innocent. That is why it works. A good FRH mystery in Cloudburg never has to manufacture atmosphere from nothing. The town already knows how to hold pressure.
